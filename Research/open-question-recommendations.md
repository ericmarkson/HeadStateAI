## Q1 — How much intra-episode granularity should be captured?

**Reviewed — REJECT AND RECONSIDER, then revised.** An adversarial QA pass rejected the first draft: the Delphi-provenance claim supporting the once-daily floor was false; the re-argument never engaged this project's own fixed clinical constants (12 h remission, 48 h separation, 30 min treated-day); the hard-flag backfill exclusion invented an uncited rule against an explicit anti-pattern; the two compliance meta-analyses were used to argue for a conclusion they are actually neutral on, with the one migraine-specific 4×/day counterexample sidelined to a parenthetical; aura wasn't unconditional; the burden curve was inverted for a status-migrainosus patient; and two dependencies (Capacitor-only scheduled notifications, instrument-wording licensing) went unnamed. The two-tier shape survives; what each tier is *for* does not.

### Recommendation: two tiers, but Tier 2 is required infrastructure, not bonus richness

**Tier 1 — the daily floor. Next-morning only; "or end-of-day" is struck.** One structured checkpoint every morning confirming the prior day/night. Two things are unconditional, asked regardless of branching: (1) aura — `DELPHI_AURA_DAY` counts an aura day as a migraine day regardless of head pain, and `migraine_days` feeds `PAYER_CGRP_EPISODIC`, so gating the aura question behind "did you have a headache" silently drops days from that count; (2) the overnight question — "did you fall asleep with a migraine and wake without one?" (the overnight rule (**not** a constant — it is the attack-scoped occupancy exception in `tech-spec.md` §5.2 step 3a; there is no `DELPHI_OVERNIGHT` constant and no `delphi_overnight` rule value)) — which only a next-morning prompt can observe, since an evening checkpoint is asked before sleep and cannot see the waking state or an attack that starts after the prompt. **On any day inside an open episode, this checkpoint is capped short** — the two unconditional questions plus a binary "still going / anything changed," nothing longer — see the burden fix below.

**Tier 2 — event-anchored open/close capture, load-bearing for the fixed constants.** This project has fixed three sub-day thresholds a once-daily, date-level checkpoint cannot resolve: `STATUS_MIGRAINOSUS_MAX_REMISSION` (12 h), `DISTINCT_ATTACK_SEPARATION` / `RECURRENCE_AMBIGUOUS_WINDOW` (48 h), and `DELPHI_TREATED_DAY_MIN` (30 min). A daily checkpoint cannot reliably distinguish a 10 h gap from a 14 h one, or a 46 h from a 50 h one — that needs roughly hour-level timestamps at the boundary, which is a **[FP]** inference from this project's own constants table, not a cited requirement (see Evidence). So:
- **Onset and resolution are timestamped at or near the moment they're noticed**, via the existing open/close affordance (npj Digital Medicine's pattern, already specified). This is the *primary* source of boundary-relevant timing — the daily floor is a backstop for days nothing else got logged, not the primary mechanism.
- **Medication doses auto-trigger targeted follow-ups** timed to pharmacology (~30–60 min and ~2 h after a triptan/NSAID; next-day for a corticosteroid taper), asking specifically about attenuation/recurrence — a dose is exactly when a remission-window or recurrence question becomes answerable, and feeds `active_pharmacology[]` and the attenuation flag directly.
- **No scheduled premonitory-window prompting**, unchanged — retrospective phase assignment remains correct per invariant 10.

**Why this doesn't run away for a status-migrainosus stretch:** a genuinely continuous, unremitting three-week episode is architecturally *low* in open/close events — one open, and by definition of "continuous," few or no closes until it ends. Event volume concentrates around the *ambiguous* cases — the 10–14 h gaps, the 46–50 h gaps — which is also when something has visibly changed and is more likely to register even through cognitive impairment. That mitigation is partial, not complete; see "what would change this."

**Passive:** effectively nothing at MVP — HealthKit and Health Connect are native-only, unreachable from a PWA. **[FP]** Post-Capacitor, treat passive sleep/HRV as an auxiliary correlate layer, not a Tier 1/2 substitute (see evidence on prediction reliability below).

**Backfill:** no penalty for entries covering today or yesterday. Beyond that, **do not exclude** — there is no published backfill-latency exclusion rule in ICHD-3 or Delphi, and inventing one is the exact anti-pattern this file's own guardrails forbid, compounded by silently dropping days from a count. Use the mechanism the project already has instead: every field carries provenance and confidence (invariant 5); a late-backfilled day is included in `headache_days`/`migraine_days` with `source: user_text` and low confidence, and that confidence travels into the export, so a reviewer sees a flagged low-confidence day rather than a silent gap or an invented cutoff. A firmer cutoff, if wanted, is a persisted preference Eric sets, not a system default — the person most likely to backfill three weeks late, mid-status-migrainosus, is also the person most harmed by an algorithm quietly deciding those weeks don't count.

### Evidence

- **[FP], corrected — this was the load-bearing error.** The prior draft claimed the Delphi definitions were derived from a once-daily, next-morning e-diary. **False, withdrawn.** The Delphi consensus (De Loose, Paemeleire, Deligianni et al., *J Headache Pain* 2026;27:135) was produced by an 18-panellist eDelphi over three rounds against a 70% agreement threshold, reasoning from clinical cases reflecting phase-III trial variation — the paper states no granularity or timing requirement at all. The correct argument for what resolution is needed comes from this project's own fixed constants, reasoned from first principles as described in Tier 2 above. Labelled **[FP]**, not **[E]**.
- **[E] Short/borderline-duration recall is least reliable — exactly where `DELPHI_TREATED_DAY_MIN` (30 min) lives.** Diary-vs-monthly-recall in headache patients ([PMC9060397](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9060397/)): monthly recall underestimated headache days by 1.7–3.4 days per 28-day window (Bland-Altman −10 to +15 days), and shorter headaches were recalled worst. This is monthly-recall evidence; extrapolating it to next-morning recall is **[FP]**, but it's the only quantified recall-degradation data found, and it resolves the prior draft's internal contradiction directly: it does not support treating a next-morning duration estimate as adequate for a 30-minute threshold, which is why treated-day duration is captured via the Tier 2 dose-triggered flow in real time, with the floor's retrospective estimate flagged lower-confidence rather than treated as equivalent.
- **[E, partially verified]** IPD meta-analysis, EMA in chronic pain ([jmir.org/2019/2/e11398](https://www.jmir.org/2019/2/e11398/); mirror [PMC6379815](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6379815)): mean completion 85%; sampling density 3–12 prompts/day **unrelated** to completion; completion declined ~2%/week regardless of frequency. Flagged partially verified: this session's own fetch of the primary URL returned empty content twice before a mirror succeeded — figures are plausible, not independently re-confirmed on a clean pass.
- **[E], reframed — this evidence is neutral, not a pillar for once-daily.** Wrzus & Neubauer, 347 EMA studies ([journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10731911211067538](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10731911211067538)): mean compliance 79.2% (SD 13.6%); scheduled assessments/day (median 6) **unrelated** to compliance. Both meta-analyses say frequency is orthogonal to compliance — that's neutral between once-daily and denser, not evidence favouring once-daily. Corrected reading: this removes the presumption that Tier 2's event-anchored density is itself a compliance risk. The real predictors found were per-entry burden, incentives, and multi-month duration decay — targeted by the burden fix (capped floor length, small real-time asks), not by minimising prompt count.
- **[E], engaged rather than parenthetical.** The one migraine-specific EMA study found — [Houle et al., PLOS ONE 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0072827) (2013, corrected from a prior miscite) — ran 4 prompts/day for 3 weeks and reached 89.5% compliance. Direct counterexample to "migraine patients can't sustain multiple logs/day": the achievable ceiling is well above one/day, at least over a few weeks in a research-motivated cohort. Does not resolve contingent-vs-fixed density, or weeks-vs-months duration — extrapolation beyond the 3-week window is **[FP]**.
- **[E]** Denser *fixed-schedule* designs decay over long horizons: 12-month burst-sampling at ~12 prompts/day during bursts ([mhealth.jmir.org/2025/1/e67117](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2025/1/e67117)) saw completion odds decline monthly (OR 0.95, p<.001), 50% attrition by 12 months. Argues against a permanent high fixed-frequency schedule, not against contingent event-triggered logging, which is architecturally different (density follows state, not a clock).
- **[E]** Once-daily reaches near-ceiling compliance in real headache populations, supporting the floor as a backstop: LUMC's e-diary (one daily alert, adaptive 3–13 questions, ~1–3 min) reached median compliance 98% (IQR 90–100%) over a median 184 days ([journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03331024251359222](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03331024251359222)); EMMA (238 patients, 6 months) logged the daily diary on 77% of days while richer behavioural modules saw rapid drop-off.
- **[E]** Passive wearable prediction is not yet reliable: a 2026 study (Empatica EmbracePlus nocturnal HRV/EDA/sleep → next-day headache; [mdpi.com/2077-0383/15/10/3802](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/15/10/3802), *J Clin Med* 15(10):3802 — corrected from a prior miscite of 2025) found AUROC 28.2%–81.2% across participants, only 1 of 9 evaluable participants reaching a clinically informative threshold. Small N; directionally supports auxiliary-only use.
- **[U], re-searched, still unresolved.** Looked specifically for same-day (not monthly) recall-bias figures in the momentary-vs-recall literature. The directly on-point papers exist — Broderick et al. 2009, "Can end-of-day reports replace momentary assessment of pain and fatigue?" ([PMC2757263](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2757263/)); Cook et al., spinal-cord-injury EOD-vs-EMA reliability, *Qual Life Res* 2018 ([link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11136-018-1952-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11136-018-1952-y)) — but full text was blocked in every attempt this session. The hour-level-precision argument above rests on the project's fixed constants, not on this literature — do not upgrade it to **[E]**.
- **[U]** N1-Headache mandates daily entry and a 90-day minimum before analysis; no published attrition rate for reaching that window was found. Open unknown, not a benchmark.

### Named dependencies this recommendation creates

- **Capacitor — for the active tier, not only passive sensing.** Tier 2's dose-triggered follow-ups, the 12 h open-episode nudge, and the next-morning floor reminder all need reliable scheduled local notifications. A PWA has no local-notification scheduling API, and iOS web push is home-screen-install-only and unreliable. A third Capacitor dependency, alongside HealthKit/Health Connect and background audio — not a clean pure-web MVP. Pre-Capacitor, expect degraded reminder reliability on iOS specifically; fallback is user-initiated logging plus an in-app open-episode banner rather than push-driven prompts.
- **Instrument licensing.** The daily checkpoint's question wording (aura, overnight, and any adaptive follow-up items) is itself a licensing surface. `CLAUDE.md` prohibits embedding PROMIS, HIT-6, MIDAS, MSQ or MPFID item wording in anything distributed or sold — a prior QA pass found PROMIS licensing was the most materially wrong item in the project so far. Wording here must be independently authored against ICHD-3/Delphi criteria language, not copied or lightly paraphrased from a licensed instrument, and needs legal review before shipping.

### Strongest counter-argument, engaged on burden, not resolution

The counter that matters is burden, not resolution: a checkpoint that grows on worse days is heaviest exactly when Eric's capacity is lowest, and LUMC's largely-episodic population doesn't test that regime. The fix is structural: the daily floor is capped short on any day inside an open episode, and the detail resolving the 12 h/48 h/30 min boundaries is pushed into small, single-purpose, near-real-time event asks (open, close, dose-response) instead of accumulating into one end-of-day form. This bounds worst-case per-interaction cost; it does not eliminate how many interactions a bad stretch can generate. Not fully proven safe — see below.

### What would change this

- Eric skipping the next-morning floor at a materially higher rate than the LUMC/EMMA populations — argues for shortening the floor further (binary only, every day), not adding prompts.
- Eric skipping or mislogging open/close events specifically during the worst days of a status-migrainosus stretch — since that's precisely when the 12 h/48 h boundary calls matter most, this would mean Tier 2 fails exactly where it's load-bearing, and would force reconsidering whether boundary determination should default to the wider, more conservative reading (the project's existing dual-reading rule under `RECURRENCE_AMBIGUOUS_WINDOW` already computes both candidates — this would mean weighting toward the reading that needs less late-episode input as the default display, not inventing a new rule).
- A retrospective audit showing reconstructed attenuation/recurrence timing disagrees materially with what Eric remembers.
- Post-Capacitor HealthKit/Health Connect data beating the 28–81% AUROC range found here — would justify shifting some Tier 1 context questions (sleep especially) to passive.
- Same-day recall-bias figures becoming retrievable and showing short-duration headaches are recalled meaningfully worse next-morning than full-day durations are — would strengthen the case for flagging the floor's retrospective duration estimates as low-confidence by default, not only when late-backfilled.

**Confidence: medium — down from the prior draft's medium-high, reflecting the withdrawn Delphi-provenance claim.** The compliance-frequency evidence (both meta-analyses, LUMC, EMMA, the 2013 4×/day study) is solid and now correctly framed as neutral-to-permissive rather than as a case for once-daily. The resolution argument (12 h/48 h/30 min needing event-level timestamps) is **[FP]**, reasoned from this project's own fixed constants, not from a cited methodological source. **[U]** N1-Headache's 90-day attrition rate and same-day (versus monthly) recall-bias figures remain unverifiable with the access available this session.

## Q2 — How should the recall summary be delivered?

**Reviewed — ACCEPT WITH AMENDMENT.** An adversarial QA pass corrected two sickle-cell statistics that were stated as percentages when they are percentage points (and flagged that study's small-n, single-centre, uncontrolled pre-post design, and its cost figure as potential rather than realised savings); reframed the AHS stigma finding to separate real concealment rates from an unmeasured drug-seeking mechanism; added missing limits to the sickle-cell analogue (no control arm, and a mechanism disanalogy with migraine); exempted the recall summary from Q4's mandatory document-confirmation gate; and added a safety subsection alongside the existing credibility discussion. The three-format recommendation and page order stand.

### Recommendation: three formats in v1, in priority order. Offline printing is a v1 requirement — but read the qualification.

1. **Print-formatted, screen-readable single page.** HTML styled for both display and `window.print()`, cached by the service worker so it renders and prints with zero network. **Offline printing is a v1 requirement** in the sense that matters: the page must render and be printable the instant a printer is available, with no network round-trip. That is a front-end constraint — static HTML/CSS, no server-rendered PDF, no live fetch — and cheap if designed in from the start. It is *not* a claim that the app can conjure paper in an ED waiting room.
2. **On-screen display**, one tap from app open, no scroll needed for the top third. This is what most real encounters will actually use.
3. **A time-boxed, single-use share link** for the caregiver-presents-for-incapacitated-patient case. Generated on demand, expiring in 24–72 hours, revocable, logged. **Not a persistent URL.**

**Deferred:** Wallet passes, QR as a primary access path, Lock Screen Medical ID integration, PDF email export, EHR-note integration.
**Not recommended at all:** a plain "share this link with your doctor" feature with no expiry, and any design depending on hospital wifi or the patient's data connection at the point of use.

### Recommended page order — and why order is the whole design
1. **Current episode status, in the largest type on the page.** "Day 12 of a continuous headache episode. 2 prior ED presentations this episode (dates). Not a new or first-time headache."
2. **Medications taken, last 72 hours** — time, drug, dose, most recent first. Plus acute-medication use as a count of the last 30 days, with the medication-overuse threshold flagged if met.
3. **Tried this episode, with result** — including corticosteroid → temporary remission → recurrence.
4. **Baseline and red flags** — typical pattern, aura status, what is different this time.
5. **Current preventives and diagnoses.**
6. **Footer** — provenance line, generation timestamp, prescribing neurologist contact.

**[FP]** Section 1 leads because the entire motivating failure was re-triaging a continuous episode as new each time; that must be corrected in the first line read or everything after it is processed under the wrong frame. Section 2 is second because medication timing is the highest-consequence, hardest-to-recall-verbally fact in an ED and is actionable within the first minute. A triage nurse gives a handed document seconds, not minutes.

### The drug-seeking dynamic — address it in provenance and wording, not by omission
- **[E] The concealment dynamic is real and migraine-specific; the drug-seeking mechanism behind it is illustrative, not measured.** A 2023 AHS-presented study (**n=40, conference presentation, single tertiary centre**) found patients conceal migraine symptoms from coworkers (70%), family (40%) and friends (48%), and identified clinicians themselves as a source of stigma. The source illustrates this concealment as connected to fear of being seen as exaggerating or drug-seeking, but that causal mechanism was not itself measured — only the concealment rates and the clinician-as-stigma-source finding are study findings. A survey of Ontario ED physicians **(2011)** found triptans were not used by 66% of respondents, opioids and NSAIDs were prescribed *more* commonly than migraine-specific therapy, only 26% used formal diagnostic criteria, and only 22% referred recidivist patients to a headache specialist.
- **[E] Documentation works — but every effective precedent is clinician-authored, and the strongest of the two studies is small and uncontrolled.** Two sickle-cell individualised pain care plan studies, both written by the patient's own haematology or pain team and placed in the chart: the first (**n=23, single centre, uncontrolled pre-post quality-improvement design**) cut length of stay by 1.23 days, 7-day readmission by **34.6 percentage points (39.1% → 4.54%)**, and the **proportion of admissions receiving IV hydromorphone fell 23 percentage points (95.7% → 72.7%)** — with a **potential**, not realised, ~$1.4M system cost impact; the second cut mean hospitalisation from 7.89 to 5.66 days (p=.024) and 30-day readmission from 59% to 25% (p<.001). NHLBI-consensus guidance frames these as a prescribing protocol written by the patient's own provider. Honest limits: the evidence is consensus-grade rather than RCT-grade, one RCT showed a non-significant signal toward *increased* revisits, about a third of eligible patients initially declined to enrol, there is **no control arm**, and there is a **mechanism disanalogy**: vaso-occlusive crisis has an objective substrate and NHLBI consensus guidance behind its care plans, migraine in the ED has neither, and the sickle-cell stigma literature is inseparably racialised in a way that does not transfer to migraine. It remains, with these limits stated, the best-handled analogue in this document.
- **The critical implication:** there is **no evidence** that a purely patient-generated, patient-controlled document reduces suspicion. **[FP]** An unusually polished self-assembled dossier handed to a suspicious triage nurse may read as sophistication rather than credibility.

Design consequences: make it read like a clinical record extract, not an advocacy brief — neutral tabular formatting, timestamps, no persuasive language. **Never request a specific drug or dose on the page.** State what was tried and what happened; let the clinician draw the prescribing conclusion. Add a provenance line — "generated from a continuously time-stamped patient log, not reconstructed from memory" — which reframes authority as logging discipline rather than self-advocacy. **Build toward, but do not require for v1, a clinician-countersigned version.** That is the mechanism with actual evidence behind it.

### Interaction with Q4's document gate — the recall summary is exempt

Q4 recommends that every unconfirmed inference feeding a "clinician summary" be force-surfaced for confirmation before the document is produced — mandatory and non-configurable. The recall summary **is** a clinician summary, but it must render in seconds, offline, one tap from app open, for a photophobic patient in an ED. An unskippable confirmation flow on that path is the same category of harm as a paywall on it. **Resolution: the recall summary is exempt from Q4's gate, with unconfirmed items visibly labelled on the page itself** — which invariant 5's per-field provenance already supports. Q4's gate applies to the prior-authorisation packet, not to this artifact.

### Mechanics
- **[E]** `window.print()` and CSS print stylesheets are client-side and work with zero network once service-worker cached. The constraint is the printer, not the app: AirPrint or Mopria plus a discoverable printer, neither of which exists in an ED waiting room. **A wallet card printed once at home is the only reliably available paper path** — worth an onboarding nudge rather than an in-the-moment feature.
- **[U] No evidence was found that ED staff routinely scan patient-presented QR codes.** Keep QR secondary, on the printed card, for a pharmacist or unhurried follow-up — not the primary ED path.
- **[E]** iOS Medical ID is reachable without unlocking, but it is a fixed-field system for the unconscious-patient/EMS case, not a dynamic 72-hour dosing table. Defer.
- **[FP]** Design the on-screen view to be screenshot-friendly, because users will screenshot it whether or not it is a feature.

### Link security
- **[E] FTC Health Breach Notification Rule** covers non-HIPAA DTC health apps and treats **unauthorised disclosure**, not just hacking, as reportable — 60 days to notify, civil penalties up to roughly $53,088 per violation as of 2025. A leaked or forwarded link is exactly this fact pattern.
- **[E] Washington My Health My Data Act** requires separate and distinct opt-in consent specifically for *sharing*, and carries a **private right of action**; the first MHMDA lawsuit was filed February 2025. It applies based on Washington residents' data, not company location.
- Therefore: an explicit separate consent screen at link-generation time, a default 24–72 hour expiry, revocability, and no link silently persisted. Here the compliance-safe and clinically-safe answers coincide — an unexpiring link is both stale data presented as current and indefinite disclosure exposure.

### Safety, not just credibility

This section has discussed credibility at length and safety not at all. The recall summary is a clinical artifact someone will act on: if an extracted medication time is wrong, an ED may withhold or duplicate a dose. Add, as v1 requirements: an **erratum/correction path** for an already-shared link, so a mistake discovered after sharing can be corrected; a clear statement of **what revocation does not undo** — a recipient who already retained or screenshotted the content is unaffected by revoking the link; a **"verify with patient" instruction** printed on the page itself, so a clinician treats it as a starting point rather than ground truth; and **third-party consent handling** for the caregiver-share case, where the person generating the link and the person it describes are not the same person. Invariant 5's per-field provenance is framed elsewhere in this section as a credibility device — here its more important job is as a **safety** device, since it is what lets an erratum or a low-confidence field actually be located and acted on.

### Strongest counter-argument
The sickle-cell results that move outcomes came from plans integrated into the institution's **own EHR**, not from patient-carried documents. Under that reading, perfecting a hand-off artifact solves a problem only clinician-side systems have been shown to solve, and HeadState should prioritise an MCP/API path so the physician's own system can pull the data. Held anyway because that describes the better long-run intervention, not an available v1 one — HeadState has no institutional relationship, no EHR integration, and Eric is one patient surviving individual visits now. The counterfactual is a symptomatic patient verbally reconstructing 72 hours of dosing under ED time pressure. EHR integration is the v2+ target and the MCP server is the right architectural seed for it.

### What would change this
Direct evidence that ED clinicians distrust patient-authored summaries relative to clinician-authored ones for pain complaints (would push toward gating behind cosignature before wide release). Evidence that triage workflows do accept phone screens or scan patient QR codes. Caregivers consistently needing access before a fresh link can be generated. Any sign MHMDA enforcement is reaching small DTC tracking apps.

**Confidence: medium.** The sickle-cell care-plan evidence is numeric but very small-n and uncontrolled; the migraine-specific stigma evidence is real but survey and qualitative rather than outcomes-based. **[U]** A Cochrane review on frequent-ED-attender care planning could not be fully accessed and is excluded rather than cited. **[U]** Several primary sources were CAPTCHA-blocked and triangulated through mirrors — numbers were consistent across independent searches but not confirmed against original PDFs. **[U] No migraine-specific study of a patient-carried document's effect on an actual ED encounter appears to exist**, which is itself informative: this would be early-mover application of a sickle-cell-proven pattern.

## Q3 — Should a forward-looking prodrome alert ship at all?

**Reviewed — ACCEPT WITH AMENDMENT.** An adversarial QA pass deleted the regulatory leg of the argument entirely (a source was found, but the finding claimed from it is not the finding it supports), marked the PRODROME NNT as endpoint-derived rather than a single quoted figure and downgraded the rescue-medication numbers to unverified, qualified both CGM figures (the 29% alarm-response figure as 2005-era overnight data, the Dexcom 36% figure as a subgroup result from a Dexcom-authored analysis), escalated two questions — whether the no-prediction guardrail permits a conditional base-rate display, and whether the surface should be default-on — for Eric's decision rather than settling them here, flagged that the feature may be undefined for a status-migrainosus patient, and noted the build-order dependency on phase annotation (step 5 of 7). The no-push recommendation survives on its other three legs.

### Recommendation: no push alert. Ship a passive, pull-based, retrospectively-framed pattern surface instead.

- **What ships, default-on:** when the user opens the app, if the day's observations match a **personally-confirmed** premonitory pattern — symptoms *this user* has previously relabelled as premonitory, not a population prior — show a passive non-blocking state. A badge on the day's timeline; on tap, one factual sentence: *"Photophobia + neck stiffness — present before 6 of your last 9 attacks, typically 6–14 hours ahead."* No push. **No future-tense language.** Purely descriptive of a historical pattern in the user's own data.
- **Cold-start gate:** show nothing until ≥8–10 retrospectively-confirmed premonitory instances exist.
- **Controls:** mute entirely; adjust the match threshold; edit which symptoms count as candidate markers — only ones the user personally confirmed, never auto-added from population data.
- **Not shipped:** any push notification claiming or implying a future attack, at any confidence level, in the commercial product.
- **Eric's personal build:** an experimental opt-in push variant behind a developer flag, off by default even for him. He is both subject and clinician-equivalent for his own n-of-1 data. **His tolerance must not set the commercial default.**

### Evidence
- **[E] There is genuinely something to do at 10 hours' notice.** The **PRODROME trial** (ubrogepant 100 mg vs placebo, dosed during a clinician-verified prodromal event, n=477 efficacy population): absence of moderate-to-severe headache at 24 h was **46% vs 29%**, OR 2.09, p<.0001; at 48 h, 41% vs 25%, OR 2.13, p<.0001. **NNT is endpoint-specific, not a single figure: ≈6 for the moderate-to-severe-headache endpoint, versus ≈10 for the any-intensity-headache endpoint — and both are per prodrome event treated, not per patient.** **[U] Rescue-medication use (previously quoted here as 21.7% vs 39.4%) is downgraded to unverified pending a primary source.**
- **[E] But the trial enrolled patients who could reliably identify their own qualifying prodromal events** — a pre-selected, trained population. That is exactly the gap the recognition data exposes. In the underlying prospective study (229 attacks, 34 patients): only **27.5% of attacks were recognised as premonitory before pain onset**; only **35.3% of patients were "good predictors"** (>50% of their own attacks); PPV was **85.1%** but only among the subset who predicted at all.
- **[E] The study's own authors conclude** that a strategy of administering treatment during the premonitory phase, based only on prior recognition of symptoms, "would benefit a very small percentage of patients." A methodological letter further cautions that PPV alone overstates real diagnostic value without ROC or train/validate splits.
- **[U] No AHS position statement endorsing patient self-triggered prodrome-phase dosing outside a research protocol could be found.** PRODROME is a single industry-funded trial not yet folded into a formal guideline recommendation. Flagged as a real evidentiary gap.
- **[E] CGM is the right analogue and cuts both ways — and both sides need qualifying.** The bad case: patients respond to only **29%** of hypoglycaemia alarms (**overnight response, on 2005-era sensors** — not current-generation hardware); parents wake to only **37%** of nighttime alerts; ~25% of caregivers become unresponsive to repeated alarms; discontinuation attributable to alarm fatigue ranges **12–62%**. **The alarm-fatigue review's own conclusion is that fatigue does not negate CGM's overall benefit — that belongs in this "cuts both ways" framing, not as an omission.** The good case: Dexcom's predictive "Urgent Low Soon" (20-minute horizon, objective sensor input, 30-minute cooldown) achieved **93% opt-in retention** and a **36% relative reduction** in hypoglycaemia exposure **in a subgroup (infrequent screen viewers), from a Dexcom-authored retrospective analysis** — not the trial's headline result. **[FP]** The difference is still precisely the variables a prodrome alert lacks — continuous objective sensor input, a validated per-patient model, a short horizon, and one crisp action. A prodrome alert has noisy self-report, an unvalidated model, a 10-hour horizon, and an ambiguous action, placing it nearer the fatigue end.
- **[E] Unvalidated forecasting is the category norm, not a validated one.** Migraine Buddy's Weather Risk Forecast publishes no accuracy figures; a Smithsonian piece quotes a co-founder claiming 90% prediction accuracy with no cited study. Claru markets a 7-Day Risk Forecast with testimonials as the only evidence offered. Shipping a careful version of the same pattern risks being read as another unvalidated forecast app regardless of the internal maths.
- **[E], corrected — the regulatory leg is deleted.** The prior draft argued the CDS carve-out and a 10-hour horizon placed a prodrome push notification at the FDA's "disfavoured end: short horizon." That argument is withdrawn. It is the clearest instance in this document of a failure mode a prior QA pass named on this project: a source was found, and the finding it supports is not the finding claimed. Specifically — the argument conceded the CDS carve-out never reached DTC software, then immediately drew a DTC conclusion from HCP-facing CDS commentary anyway; the Covington source it leaned on says FDA moved the time-critical limitation *within* the HCP criteria, explicitly **"did not address… when decisions would be considered 'time critical' or not, leaving uncertainty,"** and explicitly flags **"Silence on Consumer-Facing Tools"**; and the argument called a **10-hour horizon** "short," which it is not by any reading, and FDA never defined "time critical" at all. The honest sentence in its place: **FDA's January 2026 revision says nothing about patient-facing prediction either way; per `clinical-regulatory.md` it moved *toward* permitting risk scores in the HCP context, so "we don't predict" is a product and trust choice, not a regulatory one** — which is what `CLAUDE.md` already says. The recommendation below survives on its other three legs (the recognition-rate ceiling, the CGM fatigue analogue, and the unvalidated-forecast category norm) and should not be propped up by a retired regulatory position.
- **[U] No migraine-specific quantitative study on notification-induced anxiety or prediction anxiety was found.** The anxiety argument rests on analogy to general wearable and CGM literature — flagged as reasoning by analogy, not a migraine-population evidence base.

### Escalated for Eric's decision — not settled here

Two items here are guardrail questions, not researcher calls, and this recommendation does not resolve them:

1. **Does the no-prediction guardrail permit a conditional base-rate display?** `CLAUDE.md` says: "Do not ship attack-risk prediction or forecasting, and do not present trigger correlation as prediction. No risk scores." The proposed surface fires **because today's observations match** a personally-confirmed pattern, and displays "present before 6 of your last 9 attacks, typically 6–14 hours ahead" — a **67% conditional probability with a 6–14-hour horizon, delivered at decision time.** Past-tense grammar does not change the information content; a user reads "6 of 9" as "probably today." The guardrail's second clause — *do not present trigger correlation as prediction* — is aimed precisely at this manoeuvre. This is a guardrail question, and changing a guardrail is not a researcher's call.
2. **Should it be default-on?** The draft above specifies default-on. That compounds the first question: a guardrail-adjacent surface being on by default for every user raises the stakes of getting (1) wrong.

**This is not an instance of the retrospective-phase-linkage differentiator described elsewhere in this project.** That differentiator operates on *closed* attacks, letting a later attack reach back and relabel earlier observations. This pattern surface fires *before* the attack. Opposite direction, and a new capability — it should not be waved through on the strength of an argument made for a different mechanism.

### The feature may be undefined for its primary user

"Present before 6 of your last 9 attacks" presupposes discrete attacks with clean interictal baselines. For a status migrainosus or continuous-headache patient there may be **no computable "before an attack."** The premonitory literature this rests on is episodic migraine. The wearable-prediction study cited under Q1's evidence found **no chronic-migraine participant achieved better-than-random AUROC.** Check whether the feature is definable for Eric before building it — this may be a case where the commercial feature ships but Eric's own build cannot use it as specified.

### Build-order dependency

The pattern surface requires per-user confirmed premonitory instances — i.e. phase annotation and retrospective relabelling, which is **step 5 of 7** in the build order. The draft above says "what ships, default-on" without saying when; it should not be read as available before that dependency is built, and it further depends on Tier 1 phase labels actually reaching the review queue (see Q4(c) and the cross-cutting collisions section).

### Strongest counter-argument
PRODROME is genuinely strong — arguably one of the best acute-care results in migraine in years — and a purely passive design relies on the user opening the app during a phase when they may be foggy and *less* likely to look. The entire value of push is that it reaches you without your having to think to look. This may discard real value for the ~35% who could become good predictors, exactly the population PRODROME suggests benefits. Held anyway because the evidence justifying push is the exception, not the rule; the authors say a recognition-based strategy helps "a very small percentage"; and the best-documented analogue shows this design pattern degrades trust at plausible false-positive rates. (The regulatory argument previously made here has been withdrawn — see Evidence, above.) **Earn the right to push per-user; do not ship it as a population default.**

### What would change this
A specific user's own pattern crossing a real threshold — sensitivity >50%, PPV >80% on their own retrospectively-confirmed history, n≥15–20 confirmed instances — at which point a tunable opt-in per-user push is evidence-justified. A prospective replication showing real-world (not researcher-primed) recognition sensitivity well above ~28%, or evidence that structured logging itself *trains* recognition over time (searched; not found). A guideline statement endorsing patient self-triggered premonitory dosing. Direct evidence that predictive alerts are tolerated in headache populations rather than diabetes ones.

**Confidence: moderate-high** on the treatment-efficacy and epidemiological numbers, cross-verified across independent sources. **Lower** on migraine-specific patient-preference and anxiety data, which does not appear to exist, and on the precise regulatory classification, which would turn on exact product copy.

## Q4 — Should medication-response inference auto-resolve boundaries, or only propose?

**Reviewed — ACCEPT WITH AMENDMENT.** An adversarial QA pass found the 14-day timeout as specified is a no-op on the data (`confirmed_by` has only two values, so nothing changes when an item times out — flagged as a schema question for Eric, not resolved here), added a missing timeout-rate metric, specified Tier 1 phase tagging as strictly retrospective, named two capabilities (a `draft` episode state, a projection confidence field) the current schema does not have, reversed the draft's recommendation to relax Eric's document gate, replaced a misleadingly precise DDI confidence interval with an honest heterogeneity statement, qualified the mammography effect size as measured under a ~30% engineered error rate, and flagged collisions with Q2 and Q3 (see the rewritten Cross-cutting observations section). The tiered, batched-review shape survives.

### Recommendation: propose by default; auto-apply only a narrow class of cheaply-reversible inferences; make confirmation batched, deferred, and near-zero-cost rather than per-event and mid-attack.

**Tier 1 — auto-apply silently, logged and reviewable.** Only inferences that do not change what the user asserted, are trivially reversible, and have no export consequence yet: tagging an observation with a *candidate* phase label **— specified here as strictly retrospective: this fires only when triggered by a later attack opening, never at the moment the observation is captured, per invariant 10 and the retrospective-phase-linkage design; tagging at capture time would contradict both**; updating a projection's internal confidence; extending a *draft* open episode's provisional end time where no medication-response boundary claim is attached and no document has been generated. **Two items here assume schema the data model does not currently have: a `draft` open-episode state (the model has only `open: bool`) and an "internal confidence" field on a projection (`day_classifications` carries no confidence field — only `phase_annotations` does). Both are schema additions this recommendation assumes, not capabilities that exist today; flag for Eric's sign-off alongside the `review_status` question below.** Always marked `confirmed_by: system`, silently superseded the moment the user acts contrary to them.

**Tier 2 — queue for batched deferred confirmation. The default for anything ICHD-3-consequential.** Any medication-response episode-boundary inference — the steroid case, any 48-hour-rule application, any status migrainosus classification — is computed and **displayed immediately** as feedback while it is fresh, but **confirmation is not requested in the moment.** It sits in a review queue with a confidence badge. Confirmation happens at whichever comes first:
- **Weekly review screen** — one scrollable card list of everything inferred since last review. Accept-all, or open any card for evidence and override. If untouched after ~14 days the item is **accepted as `confirmed_by: system`, and does not silently become `confirmed_by: user`** — **though note this is a no-op on the data:** `confirmed_by` has exactly two values, and an item that times out is already `confirmed_by: system` from the moment it was computed, so passing 14 days unconfirmed changes nothing in the record. All the timeout actually does is clear the item off the review queue. See "Eric vs commercial" below for what would be required to make the distinction real.
- **Document-generation time** — every unconfirmed inference feeding a payer export, clinician summary, or LLM-interpreted output is **force-surfaced** for a yes/no/edit pass before the document is produced. This is the one place confirmation is not optional, because it is where the cost of being wrong stops being recomputable and lands in someone else's hands.

**Tier 3 — never auto-apply.** Anything already exported, anything contradicting a prior user override, anything status-migrainosus- or diagnosis-adjacent enough to read as a clinical assertion.

**This design collides with both Q2 and Q3 — see "Collisions between recommendations" in Cross-cutting observations, below.** Q2's recall summary is a clinician summary that must render instantly and offline, in tension with this section's document gate (resolved there by an explicit exemption). Q3's pattern surface depends on candidate phase labels reaching the Tier 2 review queue, which Tier 1's silent auto-apply, as scoped above, does not by itself guarantee — the labels must be routed into review even though they auto-apply.

**Confidence tiering** decides only *how loud* a Tier 2 proposal is, never whether confirmation is required. High confidence — a rule matches directly and unambiguously — gets a one-tap confirm at the top of the weekly screen. Low or ambiguous — anything inside the recurrence window, or any medication-response boundary — shows **both readings** and requires an active pick, with no silent default. **[FP] Do not learn the threshold from acceptance rate**; that metric is the automation-bias trap. Hand-set tiers from the ICHD-3 rule text and revisit using the **correction** rate, not the acceptance rate.

### How it avoids both failure modes
Nothing blocks capture — logging never triggers a modal, so the approval tap is decoupled from the capture action, which is Eric's stated worry answered directly. Against rubber-stamping: distinguishing `confirmed_by: system` (including timeout-accepted) from `confirmed_by: user` means the corpus always knows which facts were genuinely inspected, and a timeout-accept is honestly labelled as *not* a human judgement rather than manufactured consent. Batching lowers the cost of reviewing without lowering the fidelity of the signal, because the review screen foregrounds the ambiguous cases — the ones worth a correction — rather than swamping the user with uncontroversial confirmations that train reflexive acceptance.

### Evidence
- **[E] Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000)**, the canonical automation taxonomy, and more importantly their **four-stage model** (information acquisition → analysis → decision selection → action implementation), which argues automation level should be set **independently per stage**. This maps directly: detecting a candidate boundary can run at high automation; the boundary becoming a fact that flows into a document should stay low.
- **[E] Drug-drug-interaction alert override rates are consistently very high and extremely heterogeneous:** across 11 studies and 570,776 prescriptions, the pooled override rate is **~90% (I²=100%)** — at that level of heterogeneity a confidence interval is not meaningfully interpretable, so none is quoted here — and filtering to only high-priority alerts barely moved it (97.9% → 96%). Indiscriminate per-event confirmation trains dismissal, not evaluation.
- **[E] Automation bias in mammography AI (RSNA Radiology 2023):** when AI gave an *incorrect* BI-RADS suggestion, radiologist accuracy collapsed from ~80% correct to **20–46%** depending on experience — **measured under a study design where 12 of 40 cases carried deliberately incorrect AI suggestions, i.e. a ~30% engineered automation-error rate; the qualitative lesson (show evidence, not conclusions) transfers to this project, the magnitude does not.** Automation bias is severe and only partially mitigated by expertise. This is the sharpest argument that medication-response inferences — which the project's own attenuation caveat says will sometimes be wrong — must not be silently trusted, and that the UI must show **evidence, not just a conclusion**, at confirmation time.
- **[U] Gmail Smart Compose per-suggestion acceptance rate is not published**; the widely-repeated "~1 in 5" figure is not sourced to the paper. Treated as directional only.

### Eric vs commercial
For Eric: loosen Tier 1 further and allow configurable auto-confirm for stale low-stakes items — **but do not skip the document gate for exports he uses himself, reversing the prior draft's recommendation here.** He **is** the ground-truth generator the correction corpus depends on, and that corpus is named elsewhere in this project as its most durable asset; relaxing his gate removes friction at precisely the one source that produces it. His discipline is a reason to trust his confirmations when he gives them, not a reason to let the document-generation checkpoint go unconfirmed. For commercial: keep the document-generation gate **mandatory and non-configurable** — this is the regulatory-relevant surface, and letting a user configure it away is what turns decision support into diagnostic assertion in an audit.

**The prior draft asserted the "accepted-by-timeout ≠ user-confirmed" distinction should be visible in the UI so a clinician reading an export can tell the difference — but with a two-valued `confirmed_by` field there is nothing to render that from:** a timed-out item and a system-computed item nobody has ever touched hold the identical value. Making the distinction real requires a **third review state or a separate `review_status` field** — a change to the one-shape derived-object contract `CLAUDE.md` deliberately fixes (one shape for "the algorithm proposed / a human confirmed," not three) — and is therefore **Eric's decision, not a researcher's.** Either escalate a schema change for his sign-off, or state honestly, for now, that the 14-day timeout only clears the review queue and leaves no visible trace of having timed out.

### Strongest counter-argument
Deferred batching loses Eric's in-the-moment intuition, which is sharpest while he is living through the recurrence — a week later he is confirming from the system's summary rather than his memory, which is a mild form of the same automation bias the design is trying to avoid. Held anyway because interrupting mid-attack is worse on both axes, and because ICHD-3's recurrence-versus-relapse question is explicitly **not resolvable in the moment** — the ambiguity is definitional, not a knowledge gap that fades. **[FP] The mitigation:** capture the phenomenological detail as an *observation* at the time — a non-blocking dismissible "does this feel like the same headache or a new one?" at the same timestamp — then show that note alongside the inference at review time. Preserves the in-the-moment signal without making it a gate. This is a plausible mitigation, not a validated one.

### What would change this
Correction rate on Tier 2 dropping near zero for several consecutive weeks *while* ambiguous cases are still being flagged — the rubber-stamp signal; response is to force an active choice on ambiguous items, not remove review. Weekly review consistently skipped or backlog growing unboundedly — extend the timeout, do not start silently upgrading to `confirmed_by: user`. A document generated with force-surfaced items confirmed in under five seconds — add friction specifically there, because that is where reversibility runs out. Corpus too thin even with active review — add periodic **active sampling**, re-asking about a confirmed boundary or presenting a counterfactual, to generate disagreement signal deliberately. **A missing metric, added here: timeout rate.** The metrics above track correction rate and backlog size, but timing out is informationally identical to never reviewing — corpus volume grows while ground truth does not. A rising timeout rate is the actual failure signal, distinct from backlog size (a large backlog that eventually gets reviewed is fine; a large timeout rate is not) and from correction rate (which, computed only over reviewed items, ignores everything that timed out). Monitor it.

**Confidence: moderate-to-high** on the shape — tiered by reversibility, batched deferred review, mandatory gate at document generation. **[FP] Low** on the specific parameters (14-day timeout, weekly cadence, exact Tier 1/2 line); these are design guesses calibrated to one user's plausible tolerance, with no study of migraine-app usage patterns available. There is **no existing benchmark for a healthy correction rate in a single-user tool** — treat those thresholds as provisional.

## Q5 — What gets paywalled?

**Reviewed — REJECT AND RECONSIDER, then revised.** An adversarial QA pass accepted both "never paywall" conclusions and the overall sequencing shape, and rejected the pricing architecture: the free recall summary and the paid Clinical Export did not actually differ in *kind*, only in *duration* — a mechanism the same draft explicitly forbade; the remote MCP server (mandated in `CLAUDE.md`) was never addressed, and makes a paywall on any output derivable from data the app must expose for capture/recall architecturally unenforceable; the $45 record-retrieval comparable was a B2B legal-services price, not a consumer one, and the disability-appeal comparable actually argued against upfront pricing; the freemium conversion benchmark was an unsourced blog figure for the wrong metric; the price comparison omitted the closest direct competitor, who matches the proposed price while bundling more; and the RCT was doing double duty as both an ethics argument and a pricing argument, which are not the same claim. Revised below. Per the reviewer's own terms, "a well-argued 'charge for nothing yet, and here is the one thing that could be charged for later and why' is an acceptable outcome" — that is close to where this lands.

### Recommendation

**Free forever, unmetered:** logging and capture, the full observation history, and the one-page recall summary including its ED variant (view and PDF export). Unchanged from the prior draft — see "What must never be paywalled" below.

**Paid subscription, deliberately cheap, launched second:** an **Insights** tier — correlation dashboards, weather and trigger pattern analysis, coaching content — at **$4.99/month or $29.99/year**, introduced only once a user has enough data for correlation to mean anything. This is a **competitive-floor price, not an evidence-derived one** — see "What the M-sense RCT does and does not license" below for why that distinction matters. This is still the primary revenue bet, but held with lower confidence than the prior draft implied, because the same enforceability problem that kills per-document pricing weakens it too, just less severely — see "The MCP problem."

**No per-document Clinical Export at launch, and no clean version of one identified yet.** The prior draft's $12–15 pay-per-export product is **dropped as specified**. Two independent problems, either one sufficient on its own: (1) no stable *kind*-based line separates it from the free recall summary once duration-gating is (correctly) ruled out — see "The free/paid line" below; (2) the MCP server makes a paywall on data-shaped output unenforceable against exactly the users most likely to pay for it. What might become chargeable later, and why it is architecturally different from "a nicer PDF," is in "What could become chargeable later."

### What must never be paywalled
1. **[E] Capture and full history.** The M-sense RCT found the diary-only control arm improved as much or more than the full trigger-analysis app (7.76→5.41 versus 7.74→5.79 monthly migraine days; between-group difference 0.38, not significant). Self-monitoring, not analytics, was the active ingredient. **Charging for the one thing evidence says works while giving away the unproven part is backwards.** This also forecloses history-depth gating (Migraine Trail caps free history at 60 days) — capping history directly contradicts a longitudinal-correlation and day-count-documentation pitch.
2. **[E] The recall summary, including its crisis form.** A patient hitting a paywall mid-ED-visit is not a churn event, it is a harm event. The nearest cautionary precedent: Her75 hard-paywalled core value before onboarding and lost 67 App Store ranking positions in three days from review backlash. **[U] This precedent is downgraded from how the prior draft used it** — the account is a single app-analytics marketing blog, and the causal attribution (paywall → ranking collapse, rather than coincident factors) is the blog's own claim, not independently verified this session. It is directional colour, not a load-bearing data point. HeadState's version would still plausibly be worse regardless, because the moment of failure is a medical crisis rather than a review-bombing — that reasoning is **[FP]**, not evidenced by Her75 or anything else found.

### The free/paid line — does it actually hold?

Q2's free recall summary already contains most of the payer-relevant arithmetic: current-episode day count, medications with times, **acute-medication use as a 30-day count with the MOH threshold flagged**, baseline pattern, and current preventives. The prior draft's Clinical Export differed from this by (a) a longer time window and (b) documented preventive-*failure* history — which is a difference of **duration**, not **kind**. That draft simultaneously forbade duration/history-depth gating as a matter of principle (conclusion 1, above). Both cannot be true at once, and the "never paywall history" conclusion is the one that survived review — so the free artifact, as previously scoped, swallows the paid one. No amount of re-pricing fixes this; the boundary itself was never load-bearing.

The one distinction that might hold is **purpose, not depth**: the recall summary answers "what is happening right now, for someone deciding treatment in the next hour" (state-oriented, unlimited window notwithstanding, because most of its fields are inherently current — last 72 hours of medication, this episode). A payer document answers a different question: "does this patient meet *this specific named payer's* published approval criteria" (`PAYER_CGRP_EPISODIC`, `PAYER_CHRONIC_BOTOX`, `PAYER_BOTOX_CONTINUATION` in `CLAUDE.md`'s clinical constants table) — which requires maintaining and correctly applying payer policy language that differs by plan and changes over time, not simply showing more days. That is a genuine kind-difference **in principle** — value from curated, maintained external-policy matching, not value from the user's own data being shown for longer. **[FP]** This is a plausible resolution, not a proven one, and it is weaker than it looks once the MCP server is accounted for — see below.

### The MCP problem

`CLAUDE.md` mandates a remote MCP server exposing log **and query** tools over the same backend the PWA uses, so that Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Siri can read a user's own record on request. This has a direct consequence the prior draft never addressed: **any output computable from data that must already be exposed for capture and recall is reconstructable by a connected assistant in one prompt**, for free, regardless of what the in-app UI gates. If a free user's assistant can already query day classifications, drug classes and medication history to build the free recall summary, the same user can ask that assistant to "pull the last six months and check it against Cigna's Botox criteria" and get most of a paid export's substance without HeadState seeing a payment. A paywall on a *rendering* of data the backend must already serve is not a real barrier — it is a convenience tax on people who don't think to ask their assistant, which is a bad way to build a pricing strategy and a worse way to build trust.

This threatens the Clinical Export idea more than it threatens Insights, and the asymmetry is worth naming explicitly: a one-off, high-stakes document is *exactly* the kind of task a user has every incentive to get right once via a free tool they already have open — the higher the stakes, the higher the motivation to route around a $12–15 charge. An ongoing correlation dashboard is lower-stakes per instance and higher-frequency; most users will not re-prompt an assistant to rebuild a live dashboard every time they open the app, so a cheap subscription can win on convenience even though it is technically no more enforceable. **This is a friction-based moat, not an architectural one — soft, not hard — and should be described as such rather than treated as a real gate.**

The alternative — gate at the MCP query-scope layer instead of the UI layer, e.g. give free-tier assistants only recent/recall-relevant reads and require a paid scope for bulk historical queries — was considered and rejected: it reintroduces exactly the history-depth gating that conclusion 1 rules out, and it directly undercuts the "your data, your assistant" trust pitch that is job 1 (capture) and part of job 2 (recall) of the product. No clean resolution was found here; this is flagged as an open architectural tension rather than resolved.

### What could become chargeable later, and why it is different

Two candidates survive the MCP test because neither is reducible to "the user's own data, reformatted":

1. **Maintained payer-policy matching**, bundled into the subscription rather than sold per document. Checking a user's data against *specific, current* payer criteria — which vary by plan and change over time — is a curation and maintenance product, not a formatting one. A generic connected assistant does not have, and will not spontaneously acquire, a maintained database of what a specific insurer currently requires; asking one to guess risks a confidently wrong answer, which is a safety problem in a submission context, not just a business one. **[E]** This is the same failure mode the FTC found in *FTC v. DoNotPay*: the company marketed an "AI lawyer" without testing its output against a human professional's standard, and settled for $193,000 in consumer redress with a requirement to stop the "AI lawyer" framing ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-finalizes-order-donotpay-prohibits-deceptive-ai-lawyer-claims-imposes-monetary-relief-requires)). Any future HeadState document product should learn from this directly: never claim outcome equivalence to professional advocacy, and be explicit that it reflects the user's own logged data checked against a named, dated policy source, not legal or medical advice.
2. **A clinician-cosignature or review workflow**, which Q2 already identifies as the one mechanism with actual outcome evidence behind it (the sickle-cell individualised-care-plan studies — length-of-stay and readmission reductions — were all clinician-authored, not patient-generated). This is definitionally not reconstructable by a prompt, because it requires an actual clinician's signature. It is also the only version of "documents" for which Q2 found evidence that a document changes ED behaviour at all — Q2's own finding is that **no migraine-specific study of a patient-carried document's effect on an actual ED encounter appears to exist.** That gap means any future paid Clinical Export claiming clinical value, not merely convenience value, would be asserting something currently unevidenced — a claim to avoid, not a value proposition to price on.

Neither of these is a v1 product. Both point toward the existing Phase-3 clinic/B2B2C direction rather than a new consumer SKU — which is also why the sequencing below only lightly touches the earlier draft's Phase 2/3 content rather than replacing its shape.

**On demand, separated from enforceability:** the absence of a defensible mechanism is not the same as absence of demand, and it is worth being honest that real consumer willingness-to-pay for exactly this kind of document exists elsewhere. **[E]** DenialCrusher sells a one-time "Insurance Appeal Letter Packet" directly to consumers for **$39** ([denialcrusher.com](https://denialcrusher.com/articles/cost-guides)); consumer doctor's-note services (not B2B legal-record retrieval) price a single note from **~$25.99** ([drsays.com](https://www.drsays.com/home/condition-doctors-note/)). Both are genuinely consumer-paid, occasion-triggered document products, and both price *above* the prior draft's $12–15 — so the constraint on Clinical Export was never that people won't pay for paperwork. It is that HeadState cannot yet draw a line around the product that a free connected assistant can't cross for the specific users who'd pay the most.

### The revenue model to bet on

Subscription, for Insights only, at the category floor. **[FP, reframed from the prior draft]** The prior draft framed the $45 flat-fee medical-record-retrieval comparable and SSDI contingency-fee pattern as evidence that people pay for paperwork "at the moment of need." On inspection neither supports a consumer per-document price point: record retrieval at ~$45 is a **B2B legal-services price** (law firms and insurers commission it, not patients directly — [recordrs.com/pricing](https://www.recordrs.com/pricing/)), and disability-appeal help is overwhelmingly **contingency-based with no upfront cost** ([Citizens Disability](https://www.citizensdisability.com/applying-for-ssdi/are-there-any-costs-to-me-during-the-appeals-process/), [Atticus](https://www.atticus.com/advice/getting-started/how-much-does-a-disability-lawyer-cost)) — which if anything argues *against* upfront per-document pricing in adjacent categories, not for it. The genuinely consumer-paid comparables (DenialCrusher, doctor's-note services, above) are real, but they don't rescue the Clinical Export idea, because the problem with it was never pricing — it was the free/paid line and the MCP reconstruction risk. This paragraph is corrected, not merely re-labelled: the prior draft's argument for *why $12–15 was the right number* rested on comparables that don't transfer, independent of the decision to drop the product itself.

### Sequencing

Kept, with Phase 2's content adjusted to match the dropped Clinical Export and Phase 3 lightly re-pointed at the two candidates above; the ordering logic itself is unchanged from the reviewed draft.

- **Now:** free only. No paywall at launch. In a crowded floor of near-identical AI trackers with near-zero assistant-store discovery, the only available differentiator is trust — spend the early period building it.
- **Phase 1**, once there is a retained base: Insights as an opt-in upgrade, gated by data volume ("your patterns unlock after 2–3 weeks of logging") rather than a day-zero wall. Grandfather anything a free user already had; never revoke. Note explicitly to users, in the pricing copy itself, that connecting an assistant to the MCP server gives it read access to the same underlying data Insights is built from — do not imply a security boundary that doesn't exist.
- **Phase 2, revised:** no Clinical Export credit product. Instead, once the recall summary and Insights have real usage, prototype the maintained payer-policy-matching layer as an **Insights+** add-on (subscription, not per-document), and test it first with Eric's own real PA situations before any commercial claim is made about it.
- **Phase 3, 18–24+ months, contingent on scale or evidence:** unchanged in shape. **[E]** DiGA reimbursement averages **~€221 per patient** and only pays off at volume — Zanadio's ~56,000 activation-code redemptions generated ~€12M — requiring a CE mark and an RCT evidence bar HeadState does not have. RWE licensing is a real category: Evidation uses a base-fee-plus-project model, PicnicHealth is free-to-patient and monetises via pharma, and Migraine Buddy presented data from its million-user base at EAN 2018 and has a 2026 Oura data collaboration. The clinician-cosignature workflow, if it is ever built, most naturally arrives via a clinic B2B2C relationship rather than as a solo consumer feature — this is the same Phase 3 direction as before, now with a concrete product attached to it.
- **[FP] Explicitly avoid employer and disability-insurer channels**, and apply the same adversariality test to pharma RWE rather than exempting it silently (see below).

### Applying the adversariality test consistently

The prior draft excluded disability insurers on structural-adversariality grounds — data that could support a claim could also be used to contest one — but admitted pharma RWE licensing without running the same test. The honest version of that test, applied to both: a disability insurer's interest is in a **specific, identifiable individual's** eligibility for **that individual's own benefit decision** — first-party, high-stakes, and adversarial by construction. Aggregate, de-identified pharma RWE licensing (the Evidation/PicnicHealth/Migraine Buddy pattern above) is lower on the same spectrum — no single buyer is deciding whether *this* patient gets *this* approval — but it is not adversariality-free, and treating it as automatically clean would be the same mistake in miniature. **[E]** The cautionary precedent for exactly this category is *FTC v. Flo Health*: a fertility-tracking app — sensitive-condition, majority-women user base, structurally similar to HeadState's — was found to have shared health data with Facebook and Google despite promising not to, and the FTC's final order required consent changes and third-party notification ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/06/ftc-finalizes-order-flo-health-fertility-tracking-app-shared-sensitive-health-data-facebook-google)). Applying the same standard to any future RWE deal, not a lighter one: explicit, separate opt-in consent at the point of the specific data use (not buried in general terms — the same standard `CLAUDE.md` already requires for Washington's My Health My Data Act), true de-identification with no re-identification path, and named disclosure of which companies receive data. Under that standard, pharma RWE is a *later, harder-gated* option, not a materially safer one than the insurer channel — the asymmetry is one of degree, not of kind, and Phase 3 above should be read that way.

### What the M-sense RCT does and does not license

**[Corrected — the prior draft conflated two separate claims.]** The RCT licenses **"gating correlations is defensible"** — no proven clinical benefit is being withheld, so there is no ethics objection to a paywall (7.76→5.41 diary-only versus 7.74→5.79 full-feature; 0.38-day difference, not significant). It does **not** set the price, and the prior draft's $4.99 figure was dressed as if the trial supported it when the trial has nothing to say about willingness to pay. **[E]** Willingness to pay for unvalidated correlation and forecasting features is already demonstrated in-market regardless of efficacy: Migraine Trail charges **$4.99/month or $49.99/year for a 14-day barometric forecast and PDF doctor reports** ([competitive-landscape.md](/home/claude/out/Research/competitive-landscape.md); [migrainetrail.com](https://migrainetrail.com/)) — matching HeadState's proposed Insights price while bundling a document feature HeadState isn't charging for at all — and Migraine Buddy charges $7.49–12.99/month blended for weather, correlation and coaching ([migrainebuddy.com/mbplus](https://migrainebuddy.com/mbplus/)). Bearable's nominal annual is $34.99 but is **frequently discounted to $18.99** ([bearable.app/our-pricing-and-principles](https://bearable.app/our-pricing-and-principles/)) — below HeadState's proposed $29.99, a real risk to conversion that undercutting on nominal price alone won't fix. The correct framing: **$4.99/$29.99 is a competitive-floor guess, explicitly not evidence-derived, made knowing the closest direct competitor offers more at the same price and the nearest multi-condition competitor often charges less.** The RCT's actual, narrower job is constraining marketing copy — `CLAUDE.md`'s working conventions already prohibit asserting a clinical benefit for correlation features — not setting a number.

### Strongest counter-argument

Giving away logging, history and the recall summary while the one clearly monetisable idea (Clinical Export) turns out to be both legally/architecturally unstable and unenforceable may mean there is no real paid product here yet beyond a thin, easily-undercut $4.99 subscription — a genuinely weaker revenue position than the prior draft implied, not a stronger one. Held anyway, for a narrower reason than before: HeadState does not need venture-scale revenue to succeed on its own stated terms (patient-first, built for Eric's own use first), and Bearable's multi-year survival as a bootstrapped operation at this same free-core/cheap-subscription shape is at least an existence proof that the *architecture* can sustain a small operation, even though **[U] no revenue-per-install or subscriber-count figures were found for Bearable, Migraine Trail, or Migraine Buddy's MBplus specifically** to make that a quantified claim rather than a qualitative one.

### What would change this

A stable, evidenced kind-based distinction between the free recall summary and a paid document emerging later (would revive per-document pricing). Evidence that MCP-connected usage is rare enough among the free base that the reconstruction risk is theoretical rather than practical (would justify pricing Documents after all). Insights conversion coming in far below the discounted Bearable/Migraine Trail price points, suggesting $4.99 is still too high against the real competitive floor, not too low. A reachable headache-clinic partnership that makes the clinician-cosignature workflow buildable sooner than Phase 3.

**Confidence: medium-low overall, down from the prior draft's medium.** High confidence on what must stay free — logging/history and the recall summary — unchanged from review. **Low** on the Insights price point, which is now explicitly labelled competitive-floor guesswork rather than benchmarked against sustainability data that doesn't exist. **No confidence, by design** on Clinical Export pricing, because the product itself is not recommended as specified. **[U]** No app-specific willingness-to-pay survey data for migraine tracking was found; no revenue-per-install data for the named comparables was found; the WTP literature that exists concerns pharmacotherapy, not tracking tools. **[FP]** The MCP-enforceability argument and the free/paid line resolution are both reasoned from this project's own architecture documents, not from any external precedent — no comparable "paywalled feature next to a free assistant with read access to the same data" case study was found to check this reasoning against.

## Cross-cutting observations

**Three of the five recommendations converge on the same principle:** value should be delivered where the user already is, not pushed at them. The daily checkpoint replaces multi-daily polling (Q1); a pull-based pattern surface replaces a push alert (Q3); batched deferred review replaces per-event confirmation (Q4). Each was reached independently.

### Collisions between recommendations

An adversarial QA pass found four collisions between recommendations that no individual researcher noticed, plus a build-order problem (below). In order of severity:

1. **Q4's mandatory document gate lands on Q2's crisis path.** Q4 makes confirmation mandatory and non-configurable before any "clinician summary" is produced; Q2's recall summary *is* a clinician summary and must render in seconds, offline, for a photophobic patient in an ED. Resolution: exempt the recall summary, label unconfirmed items on the page, apply the gate to the prior-auth packet only. (Applied in both the Q2 and Q4 sections above.)
2. **Q4's Tier 1 starves Q3.** Q3's pattern surface opens only on symptoms the user **personally confirmed**. Q4 puts candidate phase labels in Tier 1 — auto-applied, `confirmed_by: system`, never surfaced for confirmation as specified. If phase labels auto-apply and are not routed into the Tier 2 review queue, **Q3's cold-start gate never opens and the feature is permanently silent.** Resolution: phase labels must route into the review queue even though they auto-apply.
3. **Q1 makes Q4 worse.** Q1's dose-triggered follow-ups generate more `medication_response` observations, each generating more medication-response boundary inferences — every one of which is Tier 2 by Q4's own rule. **Adopting Q1 inflates the weekly review queue that Q4's entire design exists to keep tolerable.** Neither researcher noticed.
4. **The MCP server defeats Q5's paywall.** `CLAUDE.md` mandates a remote MCP server exposing query tools over the same backend; an assistant with read access reconstructs a paid derived export from free data in one prompt. Q5 has since been revised to confront this directly, but it is recorded here as a cross-cutting architectural constraint, not a Q5-local problem.

### Build-order consequence

Three recommendations depend on Capacitor: Q1's Tier 2 scheduled notifications, Q1's passive sensing, and the EHR-integration path implied by Q2's counter-argument. Q3 depends on phase annotation at step 5 of the build order. Q4 depends on the Documents job at step 7. Q2's share link, and Q5's launch posture, both need My Health My Data Act consent architecture in v1. **The strategy brief explicitly declined a prior recommendation to pull Capacitor forward, recorded as a deliberate choice. Three of five recommendations now push against that decision.** That is a build-order revisit to be made deliberately, not a footnote.

### Evidence gaps common to several answers

No migraine-specific data exists on: patient-carried documents affecting an ED encounter; notification-induced anxiety in headache populations; willingness to pay for tracking tools; or contingent-versus-fixed prompting compliance. Several answers therefore reason from the nearest well-studied analogue — sickle cell for ED documentation, CGM for patient alerts, record retrieval for document pricing. Those analogues are defensible but should not be described as migraine evidence.

**Instrument licensing is unexamined across all five.** `CLAUDE.md` prohibits embedding PROMIS, HIT-6, MIDAS, MSQ or MPFID item wording in anything distributed or sold, and a prior QA pass found PROMIS licensing was the single most materially wrong item in the entire project. Q1 specifies a 3–15-question daily instrument and Q5 monetises the output; neither flagged that **the question wording is itself a licensing surface.** This is the exact failure that already bit this project once.

### Eric's status migrainosus changes at least three answers

Eric's own diagnosis is not a corner case for most of these recommendations. **Q1:** the daily floor cannot resolve the 12-hour remission constant that defines his diagnosis, and its adaptive checkpoint is heaviest on his worst days; he is the person most likely to backfill weeks late and most harmed by exclusion. **Q3:** the feature may be undefined for a continuous-headache patient — there may be no computable "before an attack" for him. **Q4:** his review queue will be dominated by status-migrainosus and recurrence-window items — the highest-stakes, most ambiguous class — which undermines the premise that "batching works because most items are uncontroversial." **Q5** is a no-op for the personal build, so it answers a question that does not bind for 12+ months, while Q1 and Q4 bind immediately.
